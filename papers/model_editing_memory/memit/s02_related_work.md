## 2. Related Work

**Scalable knowledge bases.** Representing world knowledge is a long-standing problem in artificial intelligence. Classical work addressed it through hand-curated knowledge bases such as `Cyc` and `WordNet`, followed by web-powered knowledge graphs such as `DBpedia`, `Freebase`, `YAGO`, `ConceptNet`, `NELL`, `Knowledge Vault`, `Wikidata`, and `COMET`. Structured knowledge bases can be precisely queried, measured, and updated, but they remain limited by sparse coverage of uncatalogued knowledge, including commonsense facts.

**Language models as knowledge bases.** Because language models can answer natural-language factual queries, prior work has proposed using them directly as knowledge bases. However, LM knowledge is implicit: outputs are sensitive to prompt phrasing, and it remains difficult to catalog, add, or update individual facts. LLMs are nevertheless attractive as knowledge stores because they scale well and are not constrained by a fixed schema. This paper focuses on the update problem: how implicit knowledge encoded in parameters can be mass-edited.

**Hypernetwork knowledge editors.** Several meta-learning methods edit model knowledge by predicting parameter updates. Sinitsin et al. train models to become editable by gradient descent. De Cao et al. propose a **Knowledge Editor (KE)** hypernetwork that predicts updates conditioned on new factual statements. Hase et al. find that KE struggles beyond a few edits and scale an improved objective to 10 beliefs. **MEND** infers weight updates from the gradient of the inserted fact, and **SERAC** routes rewritten facts through a separate parameter set while leaving original weights unchanged, scaling to 75 edits. MEMIT differs by using direct parameter updates from an explicitly computed associative mapping rather than meta-learning.

**Direct model editing.** MEMIT builds most directly on interpretability work that localizes internal mechanisms in LLMs. Following observations that transformer MLP layers behave as key-value memories, the paper focuses on MLP modules. It uses **causal mediation analysis** to identify a range of layers involved in factual recall. Previous methods such as Dai et al. and Yao et al. modify sparse neuron sets; ROME treats a GPT MLP as a linear associative memory and updates it for one fact. MEMIT generalizes this direct-editing view by modifying a sequence of layers and performing thousands of edits simultaneously.
