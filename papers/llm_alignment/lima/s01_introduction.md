## Abstract

Large language models are typically trained in two stages: unsupervised pretraining over raw text, followed by instruction tuning and/or reinforcement learning to align model behavior with user tasks and preferences. LIMA asks how much of the final assistant behavior really comes from the second stage. The authors fine-tune a `65B` parameter `LLaMa` model with the standard supervised loss on only **1,000** carefully curated prompts and responses, without reinforcement learning or human preference modeling.

Despite this minimal alignment data, LIMA learns to follow specific response formats from only a handful of examples and handles complex requests ranging from trip planning to alternate-history speculation. It also generalizes to tasks that do not appear in the training set. In a controlled human study, LIMA responses are equivalent or preferred to `GPT-4` in **43%** of cases, to `Bard` in **58%**, and to `DaVinci003` in **65%**. The central conclusion is that almost all knowledge and capabilities in large language models are learned during pretraining; limited instruction data can be enough to teach the model how to surface those capabilities in a high-quality assistant style.

## 1. Introduction

Language models pretrained to predict the next token at very large scale learn general-purpose representations that transfer to many language understanding and generation tasks. Prior alignment methods mainly focus on **instruction tuning** over large multi-million-example datasets and, more recently, **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (**RLHF**) collected through large-scale human annotation. These methods require substantial compute and specialized data to reach ChatGPT-level behavior.

The paper argues for a simpler view. Given a strong pretrained model, alignment may mostly teach the model the style or interaction format that exposes knowledge already acquired during pretraining. This is formalized in Section 2 as the **Superficial Alignment Hypothesis**. Under this hypothesis, one does not need millions of alignment examples; a small, diverse, high-quality demonstration set should be enough to tell the pretrained model how to behave as an assistant.

To test this, the authors curate **1,000** examples approximating real user prompts paired with high-quality responses. Roughly 750 examples come from public community data—`Stack Exchange`, `wikiHow`, and `r/WritingPrompts`—selected for quality and diversity. Another 250 examples are manually authored or adapted to enforce a consistent assistant style. LIMA is then trained by supervised fine-tuning from `LLaMa-65B` on this dataset.

The evaluation compares LIMA with state-of-the-art language models and products on **300** challenging test prompts. In human preference evaluation, LIMA outperforms OpenAI `DaVinci003`, which was trained with RLHF, and an `Alpaca-65B` reproduction trained on **52,000** examples. Humans still usually prefer `GPT-4`, `Claude`, and `Bard`, but LIMA remains equal or better in a substantial fraction of cases: **43%** versus `GPT-4`, **46%** versus `Claude`, and **58%** versus `Bard`. A separate absolute analysis finds that **88%** of LIMA responses meet prompt requirements and **50%** are considered excellent.

The paper also reports ablations showing diminishing returns from scaling data quantity without increasing prompt diversity, and sizable gains from improving data quality. Finally, even though the training data contains no dialogue examples, LIMA can sometimes conduct coherent multi-turn dialogue; adding only **30** hand-crafted dialogue chains dramatically improves this behavior. Together, these findings support the claim that the bulk of assistant capability comes from pretraining, while alignment data primarily selects a behavioral subdistribution.
