# How much do language models memorize?
arXiv: 2505.24832 | John X. Morris et al. (FAIR at Meta / Google DeepMind / Cornell University / NVIDIA, 2025)
Code: —

## Files

- `s01_front_matter.md` — States the paper’s central claim: model knowledge about a datapoint can be separated into **unintended memorization** of a particular dataset and **generalization** about its generating process. On synthetic independent data this yields a direct capacity measurement of roughly **3.6 bits per parameter** for GPT-style Transformers; on text it exposes the transition from memorization to generalization.
- `s02_introduction.md` — Motivates why parameter counts alone do not explain the data volume used in modern pretraining, and frames compression as an operational notion of what a model knows. It introduces the paper’s empirical program: controlled synthetic data, deduplicated real text, capacity-relative double descent, and membership-inference scaling laws.
- `s03_memorization.md` — Develops the formal definition of sample-level unintended memorization. It first gives a statistical mutual-information decomposition, then switches to Kolmogorov complexity so the quantity can be approximated from a single model and a single datapoint via arithmetic coding and model likelihoods.
- `s04_capacity.md` — Defines the capacity of a learning algorithm as its maximum total memorization and measures it on uniformly random token sequences, where generalization is impossible. Across GPT-2-style models from roughly 100K to 20M parameters, memorization plateaus at about 3.5–3.6 bits per parameter; moving from `bfloat16` to `float32` yields only a modest capacity increase.
- `s05_generalization.md` — Repeats the controlled experiments on perfectly deduplicated `FineWeb` text and separates sample-specific information from broadly useful predictive ability using a high-capacity reference model. It finds that model capacity is filled before test loss improves, so the dataset-to-capacity ratio predicts the onset of double descent; rare-token documents receive the strongest unintended memorization.
- `s06_membership.md` — Connects capacity and dataset size to loss-based membership inference. A sigmoidal law predicts F1 from the capacity-to-data ratio, and its predictions transfer from small models to `GPT2-Medium` and `GPT2-XL`; contemporary token-per-parameter ratios imply average-case F1 near chance.
- `s07_related_conclusion.md` — Situates the work among compression, neural capacity, information-theoretic learning, and extraction-based definitions of memorization. The conclusion emphasizes that the framework measures bits known about a dataset, predicts privacy-relevant membership behavior, and clarifies what capacity means under controlled conditions.
- `s08_references.md` — Full 57-entry bibliography covering information theory, memorization, capacity, extraction, membership inference, and scaling laws.
- `s09_appendix_a.md` — Supplies robustness checks, distributional analyses, scaling-law fit details, proofs of the main propositions, and limitations. The reported linear capacity approximation has average errors of 1.7–1.8%, while text experiments show that rare and non-English tokens are disproportionately memorized.
- `s10_appendix_b.md` — Compares the proposed measure with stability, extraction, membership/attribute-inference, and data-copying notions. It argues that those alternatives either rely on the training algorithm, conflate generalization with memorization, or fail to assign a stable sample-level quantity.
